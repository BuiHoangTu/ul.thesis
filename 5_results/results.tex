\section{Vanilla Autoencoder}
\label{sec:vanilla-ae-results}
For vanilla autoencoder, the concerns are mainly focused on the reconstruction quality of normal data and the ability to distinguish anomalies based on reconstruction errors.

The \gls{auc} of $0.5036$ indicates that the model does not effectively distinguish between the two classes, as it is close to random guessing.
The per-feature AUCs also suggest that some features provide a strong discrimination.
It is easy to see that stable features such as air density and temperature have higher AUCs.

Given this poor separation, I further examined how reconstruction errors for normal and abnormal samples evolved throughout training (Figure~\ref{fig:vanilla_epoch_err}).
Interestingly, the gap between the two classes is largest at around the 20th epoch, suggesting that the model initially learns to capture patterns specific to normal operating conditions while struggling to reconstruct abnormal signals.
However, as training progresses, the reconstruction error for abnormal samples decreases to match that of normal samples. 
This convergence implies that the model gradually learns to represent both normal and abnormal patterns equally well, effectively "overfitting" to the anomalies present in the training set - the data that is nearly an abnormality. 
Consequently, the discriminative signal in reconstruction error is lost, explaining the near-random \gls{auc} observed in Table~\ref{tab:vanilla_feat_auc}.

For the vanilla \gls{ae}, one of the most important design choices is the size of the latent space. 
A large latent dimension allows the network to retain more information about the input, but risks allowing abnormal patterns pass through. 
Conversely, an overly small latent dimension forces excessive compression, potentially discarding information essential for reconstructing normal behaviour. 
Both extremes can lead to poor separation between normal and abnormal samples.

To investigate this, I performed a latent size sweep, evaluating the model's performance across various latent dimensions. 
The results, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:vanilla-latent-search}, show a non-linear relationship between latent size and detection performance. 
Performance is poor at both very small and very large latent sizes.

This result suggests that either the difference in reconstruction error is highly non-linear and not captured by a simple threshold, or that the vanilla \gls{ae} lacks the representational capacity for effective Normal Behaviour Modeling in this dataset.


\subsection{Classification Net from Reconstruction Errors}
While tuning the latent size yields a modest improvement, the best \gls{auc} remains insufficient for reliable detection. 
To address this limitation, I next investigate whether a separate classifier trained on the reconstruction error vectors can better exploit potential non-linear separability between normal and abnormal samples.

For this experiment, the reconstruction errors of each sample in the training set are used as inputs to a \gls{mlp} classifier.
The \gls{mlp} consists of a single hidden layer with 64 units and a ReLU activation, followed by a single output neuron representing the probability of normal vs. abnormal operation.
The model is trained using cross-entropy loss on the labeled data.

\section{Multi-Head Autoencoder}
\label{sec:multi-head-results}
The multi-head autoencoder extends the vanilla \gls{ae} by adding a classification head to the latent space, presented in Table~\ref{tab:model-variants}. 
The hypothesis is that the supervised head will encourage the latent space to separate normal and abnormal samples, which can improve anomaly detection both directly (through the classification head) and indirectly (via reconstruction-based methods).

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{m_head-norm-abn-over-epoch.pdf}
    \caption{Multi-head autoencoder Normal vs Abnormal Reconstruction Error over epochs.}
    \label{fig:multi-head-norm-abn-over-epoch}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:multi-head-norm-abn-over-epoch} shows the tendency to separate normal and abnormal samples throughout training process.
The gap between the two classes kept increasing until the end of training, indicating that the model is learning to distinguish between normal and abnormal samples effectively.

Unlike the vanilla \gls{ae}, this model has an extra classification head that has potential to act as the anomaly detector.
This leads to a small decrease in reconstruction quality from $0.0306$ to $0.0806$, as the model is now optimizing for both reconstruction and classification objectives.
This small decrease is acceptable, as the model is still able to reconstruct normal samples with high fidelity.

\section{Non Autoencoder Models}
\label{sec:not-ae-models}
To better understand the limitations and justify the design of the multi-head autoencoder and vanilla \gls{ae} models, I examine three alternative architectures: a classifier-only model without a decoder, and two bottleneck variants (with and without the autoencoder capabilities) as shown in Table~\ref{tab:model-variants}.
These models serve to highlight the importance of the competing objectives between classification and reconstruction heads, and the role of the autoencoder in capturing meaningful representations for anomaly detection.


\subsection{Classifier-Only Model}
This model derives its architecture from the multi-head \gls{ae} but has its decoder removed entirely making it a normal classifier that operates directly on the input data.
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:multi-head-results}, the multi-head \gls{ae} balances multiple objectives—reconstruction and classification—which this model does not.
This subsection aims to evaluate the performance of a model that only focuses on classification without the reconstruction objective.

(1) The absence of a reconstruction objective reduces the quality of the learned representation leading to degraded classification performance;
(2) The relationship from the reconstruction head is supportive to the classification head, acting as a regularizer that encourages meaningful latent features that support classification.



\subsection{Bottleneck Autoencoder}
One limitation of both the vanilla \gls{ae} and multi-head \gls{ae} models, as demonstrated in Sections~\ref{sec:vanilla-ae-results} and \ref{sec:multi-head-results}, is that the reconstruction error alone does not provide a linearly separable feature space for anomaly detection.
Both models rely on a separate classifier trained on frozen reconstruction errors to achieve stable and improved performance.

To address this, two variants of the bottleneck architecture are investigated: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bottleneck AE}: This model integrates the classification loss directly into the training objective, enabling end-to-end learning that jointly optimizes reconstruction and classification in a single phase.
    \item \textbf{Bottleneck No AE}: This variant removes the reconstruction objective, focusing solely on the classification task.
\end{itemize}

    \midrule

    Recall & 0.9936 & 0.9768 & 0.9117 \\
    Precision & 0.9929 & 0.9844 & 0.8772 \\

    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

The results in Table~\ref{tab:bottleneck-comparison} clearly show that both bottleneck variants outperform the multi-head \gls{ae} by a substantial margin across all metrics.
The \textit{Bottleneck \gls{ae}} achieves the highest performance, with an almost perfect \gls{auc} of $0.9983$ and corresponding precision, recall, and F1-scores above $0.99$.
This indicates that jointly optimizing reconstruction and classification in a strongly compressed latent space yields highly discriminative representations for anomaly detection.

The \textit{Bottleneck No \gls{ae}} variant also performs very well, but its results are consistently lower than the full bottleneck \gls{ae}, with drops of approximately $1.6\%$ in accuracy and $0.22\%$ in \gls{auc}.
This performance gap may indicate that the reconstruction objective contributes meaningful regularization, guiding the latent space toward features that are both representative of normal data and sensitive to anomalies.
However, this statement needs further investigation, as the small difference may also be due to the inherent variability in model training and evaluation.
In brief, the reconstruction objective in both multi-head \gls{ae} and bottleneck \gls{ae} models provides no negative impact on classification performance, and in fact, it may help to improve the quality of the learned representations.

The \textit{Multi-Head \gls{ae}}, while competitive in earlier comparisons, lags behind both bottleneck variants here.
This suggests that the reconstruction error space contains more useful and easier-to-extract information compared to the sample data alone.


\section{Anomaly Reinforced Autoencoder}
Supervised approaches—whether directly supervised, as in the bottleneck variants, or indirectly supervised, as in reconstruction-error-based classifiers—achieve high \gls{auc} scores on the validation set.
However, these methods share a key limitation: they are trained to distinguish between normal data and a fixed set of known abnormal conditions. 
In real-world deployments, many anomalies will not appear in the training data, and models tuned to specific, known abnormal patterns may fail to generalize to unseen faults.

To address this, I propose the \textit{\gls{arae}}.
The model shares the same structure as the Vanilla \gls{ae} with the difference lying in the training process.
The core idea is to explicitly shape the reconstruction objective so that the model maximizes the reconstruction error for abnormal samples, while simultaneously minimizing it for normal samples.
In this formulation, anomalies do not simply appear as poorly reconstructed by chance; instead, the model is actively guided to produce reconstructions that accentuate deviations for abnormal inputs, as presented by Equation~\ref{eq:reinforced-loss} in Subsection~\ref{subsec:training-strategies}
This reinforcement mechanism aims to improve robustness and detection performance, even for anomaly types not encountered during training.

\section{Impact of Loss Functions}
\label{sec:loss-functions}
The choice of loss functions significantly influences the quality of latent representations in autoencoder-based anomaly detection.
Other models rely heavily on classifier \gls{mlp} to detect anomalies making the reconstruction error less important.
In contrast, the \gls{arae} framework focuses on reconstruction error as the primary anomaly signal, necessitating careful consideration of the loss function used to train the model.

In this section, I investigate the impact of several auxiliary loss terms — \gls{mmd}, Wasserstein distance, Fourier-based, and Mahalanobis distance losses — each combined with the standard \gls{mse} reconstruction loss.
The weight of each auxiliary loss is tuned to balance its contribution against the \gls{mse} loss, as shown in Equation~\ref{eq:auto-weight-loss}.
Table~\ref{tab:arae-loss-aug-results} reports the evaluation metrics obtained using a 1:2 abnormal-to-normal training ratio, illustrating how these loss augmentations affect detection accuracy.

Wasserstein achieves the highest balanced F1-score, suggesting that its optimal transport formulation encourages a more discriminative latent space without excessively distorting reconstruction quality.
