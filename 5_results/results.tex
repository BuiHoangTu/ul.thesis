\section{Vanilla Autoencoder}
\label{sec:vanilla-ae-results}
For vanilla autoencoder, the concerns are mainly focused on the reconstruction quality of normal data and the ability to distinguish anomalies based on reconstruction errors.


\subsection{Reconstruction Quality on Normal Data}
To evaluate the reconstruction ability of the vanilla AE on normal operating conditions, we compute several standard metrics: mean average error (MAE), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), maximum reconstruction error, minimum reconstruction error, maximum min discrepancy and Wasserstein distance across time windows.

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\caption{Reconstruction metrics on normal validation data .}
\label{tab:rec_metrics_normal}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
MAE                  & 0.0306 \\
PSNR [dB]            & 24.72 \\
Max error            & 0.1066 \\
Min error            & 0.0070 \\
Max--Min discrepancy & 0.0000 \\
Wasserstein distance & 0.0076 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Overall, the reconstruction errors on normal validation data are low across all evaluated metrics, indicating that the vanilla AE learns to replicate healthy input signals with high fidelity. The mean absolute error (MAE) of $0.0306$ corresponds to a small deviation relative to the signal range, while the peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) of $24.72$\,dB suggests a strong similarity between reconstructed and original signals. The maximum observed reconstruction error ($0.1066$) remains an order of magnitude smaller than typical anomaly-induced deviations, and the minimum error ($0.0070$) confirms that some time windows are reconstructed almost perfectly. The near-zero Max--Min discrepancy implies a consistent reconstruction quality across time windows, and the small Wasserstein distance ($0.0076$) shows that the overall error distribution closely matches that of the original data. These results confirm that the AE is capable of accurately modeling normal operating conditions.



\subsection{Normal vs. Abnormal Reconstruction}
For anomaly detection, the most straightforward approach is to use a static threshold on reconstruction error. For this study, AUC is first used to determine whether a significant difference exists in the reconstruction error between normal and abnormal samples.

Figure~\ref{fig:vanilla_norm_abnorm_reconst_hist}, Figure~\ref{fig:vanilla-norm-abnorm-roc} and Table~\ref{tab:vanilla_feat_auc} show that the vanilla AE is unable to create a linear separation between normal and abnormal samples based on reconstruction error. The AUC of $0.5036$ indicates that the model does not effectively distinguish between the two classes, as it is close to random guessing. The per-feature AUCs also suggest that some features provide a strong discrimination. It is easy to see that stable features such as air density and temperature have higher AUCs. These features are easier to reconstruct, thus, they are also more sensitive to anomalies.
However, they also did not provide enough information to achieve a meaningful separation overall.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering

\end{figure}

Given this poor separation, I further examined how reconstruction errors for normal and abnormal samples evolved throughout training (Figure~\ref{fig:vanilla_epoch_err}). Interestingly, the gap between the two classes is largest at around the 20th epoch, suggesting that the model initially learns to capture patterns specific to normal operating conditions while struggling to reconstruct abnormal signals. However, as training progresses, the reconstruction error for abnormal samples decreases to match that of normal samples. 
This convergence implies that the model gradually learns to represent both normal and abnormal patterns equally well, effectively "overfitting" to the anomalies present in the training set - the data that is nearly an abnormality. 
Consequently, the discriminative signal in reconstruction error is lost, explaining the near-random AUC observed in Table~\ref{tab:vanilla_feat_auc}.

For the vanilla AE, one of the most important design choices is the size of the latent space. 
A large latent dimension allows the network to retain more information about the input, but risks allowing abnormal patterns pass through. 
Conversely, an overly small latent dimension forces excessive compression, potentially discarding information essential for reconstructing normal behaviour. 
Both extremes can lead to poor separation between normal and abnormal samples.

To investigate this, I performed a latent size sweep, evaluating the model's performance across various latent dimensions. 
The results, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:vanilla-latent-search}, show a non-linear relationship between latent size and detection performance. 
Performance is poor at both very small and very large latent sizes.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{search-compression-vanilla_model.pdf}
    \caption{AUC vs latent size for vanilla AE.}
    \label{fig:vanilla-latent-search}
\end{figure}

This result suggests that either the difference in reconstruction error is highly non-linear and not captured by a simple threshold, or that the vanilla AE lacks the representational capacity for effective Normal Behavior Modeling in this dataset.


\subsection{Classification Net from Reconstruction Errors}
While tuning the latent size yields a modest improvement, the best AUC remains insufficient for reliable detection. 
To address this limitation, I next investigate whether a separate classifier trained on the reconstruction error vectors can better exploit potential non-linear separability between normal and abnormal samples.

For this experiment, the reconstruction errors of each sample in the training set are used as inputs to a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) classifier.  
The MLP consists of a single hidden layer with 64 units and a ReLU activation, followed by a single output neuron representing the probability of normal vs. abnormal operation. The model is trained using cross-entropy loss on the labeled data.

\section{Multi-Head Autoencoder}
\label{sec:multi-head-results}
The multi-head autoencoder extends the vanilla AE by adding a classification head to the latent space, presented in Table~\ref{tab:model-variants}. 
The hypothesis is that the supervised head will encourage the latent space to separate normal and abnormal samples, which can improve anomaly detection both directly (through the classification head) and indirectly (via reconstruction-based methods).

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{m_head-norm-abn-over-epoch.pdf}
    \caption{Multi-head autoencoder Normal vs Abnormal Reconstruction Error over epochs.}
    \label{fig:multi-head-norm-abn-over-epoch}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:multi-head-norm-abn-over-epoch} shows the tendency to separate normal and abnormal samples throughout training process.
The gap between the two classes kept increasing until the end of training, indicating that the model is learning to distinguish between normal and abnormal samples effectively.

Unlike the vanilla AE, this model has an extra classification head that has potential to act as the anomaly detector.
This leads to a small decrease in reconstruction quality from $0.0306$ to $0.0806$, as the model is now optimizing for both reconstruction and classification objectives.
This small decrease is acceptable, as the model is still able to reconstruct normal samples with high fidelity.
Next subsection evaluates the classification performance of the multi-head autoencoder.


\subsection{Latent Space Size Analysis}
Similar to the vanilla AE, we perform a sweep over latent space sizes to investigate their impact on anomaly detection performance for the multi-head AE. 
At each latent size, 30 models are trained and its reconstruction error is evaluated on the validation set.
The AUC is computed for each latent size, and the box plot results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:multi-head-latent-search}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.2\textwidth]{search-compression-classify_model.pdf}
    \caption{AUC vs latent size for multi-head AE.}
    \label{fig:multi-head-latent-search}
\end{figure}

The results show that having a large the compression (low number of latent channels), the linear separation between normal and abnormal samples is not feasible.
The best performance is achieved with compression rate around 1.22. However, a lot of outliers are present, indicating that the raw reconstruction error is reliable enough for anomaly detection.

While the classification head benefits from direct supervision, it is not explicitly optimized to maximize reconstruction error separation.

\section{Non Autoencoder Models}
\label{sec:not-ae-models}
To better understand the limitations and justify the design of the multi-head autoencoder and vanilla AE models, I examine three alternative architectures: a classifier-only model without a decoder, and two bottleneck variants (with and without the autoencoder capabilities) as shown in Table~\ref{tab:model-variants}.
These models serve to highlight the importance of the competing objectives between classification and reconstruction heads, and the role of the autoencoder in capturing meaningful representations for anomaly detection.


\subsection{Classifier-Only Model}
This model derives its architecture from the multi-head AE but has its decoder removed entirely making it a normal classifier that operates directly on the input data.
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:multi-head-results}, the multi-head AE balances multiple objectives—reconstruction and classification—which this model does not.  
This subsection aims to evaluate the performance of a model that only focuses on classification without the reconstruction objective.

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering

\label{tab:classifier-only-metrics}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Classifier-Only} & \textbf{Multi-Head AE} \\
    \midrule
    Accuracy & 0.8485 & 0.8610 \\
    Recall & 0.8956 & 0.9117 \\
    Precision & 0.8726 & 0.8772 \\
    F1-score & 0.8839 & 0.8942 \\
    AUC & 0.9261 & 0.9288 \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Interestingly, the result presented in Table~\ref{tab:classifier-only-metrics} shows that the classifier-only model exhibits degraded anomaly detection performance compared to the multi-head AE, confirming that:
(1) The absence of a reconstruction objective reduces the quality of the learned representation leading to degraded classification performance;
(2) The relationship from the reconstruction head is supportive to the classification head, acting as a regularizer that encourages meaningful latent features that support classification.



\subsection{Bottleneck Autoencoder}
One limitation of both the vanilla AE and multi-head AE models, as demonstrated in Sections~\ref{sec:vanilla-ae-results} and \ref{sec:multi-head-results}, is that the reconstruction error alone does not provide a linearly separable feature space for anomaly detection.  
Both models rely on a separate classifier trained on frozen reconstruction errors to achieve stable and improved performance.

To address this, two variants of the bottleneck architecture are investigated: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bottleneck AE}: This model integrates the classification loss directly into the training objective, enabling end-to-end learning that jointly optimizes reconstruction and classification in a single phase.
    \item \textbf{Bottleneck No AE}: This variant removes the reconstruction objective, focusing solely on the classification task.
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\caption{Comparison of classification metrics for bottleneck AE.}
\label{tab:bottleneck-comparison}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Bottleneck AE} & \textbf{Bottleneck No AE} & \textbf{Multi-Head AE} \\
    \midrule
    Accuracy & 0.9913 & 0.9751 & 0.8610 \\
    Recall & 0.9936 & 0.9768 & 0.9117 \\
    Precision & 0.9929 & 0.9844 & 0.8772 \\ 
    F1-score & 0.9932 & 0.9806 & 0.8942 \\
    AUC & 0.9983 & 0.9961 & 0.9288 \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The results in Table~\ref{tab:bottleneck-comparison} clearly show that both bottleneck variants outperform the multi-head AE by a substantial margin across all metrics.  
The \textit{Bottleneck AE} achieves the highest performance, with an almost perfect AUC of $0.9983$ and corresponding precision, recall, and F1-scores above $0.99$.  
This indicates that jointly optimizing reconstruction and classification in a strongly compressed latent space yields highly discriminative representations for anomaly detection.

The \textit{Bottleneck No AE} variant also performs very well, but its results are consistently lower than the full bottleneck AE, with drops of approximately $1.6\%$ in accuracy and $0.22\%$ in AUC.  
This performance gap may indicate that the reconstruction objective contributes meaningful regularization, guiding the latent space toward features that are both representative of normal data and sensitive to anomalies.  
However, this statement needs further investigation, as the small difference may also be due to the inherent variability in model training and evaluation.
In brief, the reconstruction objective in both multi-head AE and bottleneck AE models provides no negative impact on classification performance, and in fact, it may help to improve the quality of the learned representations.

The \textit{Multi-Head AE}, while competitive in earlier comparisons, lags behind both bottleneck variants here.  
This suggests that the reconstruction error space contains more useful and easier-to-extract information compared to the sample data alone.


\section{Anomaly Reinforced Autoencoder}
Supervised approaches—whether directly supervised, as in the bottleneck variants, or indirectly supervised, as in reconstruction-error-based classifiers—achieve high AUC scores on the validation set.  
However, these methods share a key limitation: they are trained to distinguish between normal data and a fixed set of known abnormal conditions. 
In real-world deployments, many anomalies will not appear in the training data, and models tuned to specific, known abnormal patterns may fail to generalize to unseen faults.

To address this, I propose the \textit{Anomaly Reinforced Autoencoder (ARAE)}.
The model shares the same structure as the Vanilla AE with the difference lying in the training process.
The core idea is to explicitly shape the reconstruction objective so that the model maximizes the reconstruction error for abnormal samples, while simultaneously minimizing it for normal samples.  
In this formulation, anomalies do not simply appear as poorly reconstructed by chance; instead, the model is actively guided to produce reconstructions that accentuate deviations for abnormal inputs.
This reinforcement mechanism aims to improve robustness and detection performance, even for anomaly types not encountered during training.

Overall, these results suggest that while the ARAE may not match the peak performance of the bottleneck AE when abundant abnormal data is available,  
its ability to operate effectively with fewer abnormal samples makes it a promising candidate for low-data anomaly detection settings.


\section{Multi-scale Attention Memory}
In this section, I aim to explore the potential of a more complex architecture that combines the strengths of both autoencoders and attention mechanisms across three models: 
(1) multi-head autoencoder - which gives quite good results with a relative large space to improve; 
(2) bottleneck with autoencoder - which achieves the best performance in this study; 
(3) anomaly reinforced autoencoder - the approach that is more favorable for detecting unseen anomalies.

The adaptations are presented in Subsections~\ref{subsec:encoder},~\ref{subsec:latent}, and~\ref{subsec:decoder}, including MAMA adaptations to the encoder, latent space, and decoder, respectively.
The comparison of the baseline models and their MAMA-adapted variants is presented in Table~\ref{tab:mama-adapted-results}.

For the Bottleneck AE, the baseline already achieves near-perfect performance (Accuracy $\approx 0.99$, F1-score $\approx 0.99$), leaving little headroom for improvement. 
Introducing the mama-adapted encoder–decoder alone causes a moderate performance drop, and adding the full adaptation (encoder, decoder, and latent space) degrades the results substantially. 
This suggests that for a well-suited bottleneck model, the proposed architectural changes may disrupt the latent space.
Especially, with more adaptations, the worse the performance becomes.

The Multi-Head AE shows only minor performance variation under adaptation. Both the encoder–decoder adaptation and the full adaptation lead to a small but consistent decline across all metrics. Given that the multi-head setup already benefits from a reconstruction-driven regularization effect, the added complexity of the proposed components may not translate into better feature representations for this architecture.

In the case of the ARAE, which is trained with the anomaly-reinforcement objective, the effect of the adaptations is more pronounced—and negative. For both the 1:2 and 1:4 abnormal-to-normal ratios, the adapted encoder–decoder reduces performance, while the full adaptation yields a larger decline in every metric. The degradation is particularly severe for recall in the low-abnormality (1:4) case, where the ARAE relies heavily on stable reconstruction dynamics that appear sensitive to architectural alterations.

Overall, the results indicate that the mama-adapted components are not universally beneficial when directly swapped into existing high-performing designs. Their integration seems to require re-tuning or model-specific adaptation, especially when the baseline is already near-optimal.
