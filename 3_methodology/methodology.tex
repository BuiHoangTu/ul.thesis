This chapter presents the methodology used to explore the capacity of Deep Reconstructive Normal Behaviour Models in wind turbine condition monitoring. The proposed pipeline is designed to detect abnormal conditions based on telemetry data from real-world operational turbines. The methodology includes data preprocessing, model design, training setup, evaluation strategy, and deployment considerations.
To ensure experimental reproducibility, all procedures involving stochastic components—such as data splitting, model initialization, and training—were executed using a fixed random seed. Unless otherwise stated, the seed value was set to \texttt{17} throughout this study.


% === Data Acquisition and Preprocessing ===
\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

The proprietary dataset used in this research was provided by EnergyPro, an industrial partner aiming to bridge academic research and industrial applications. 

The dataset initially comprised 33 variables collected from operational wind turbines, encompassing a mix of physical sensor readings, control parameters, and static metadata. These features were recorded at a nominal resolution of 10-minute intervals, though irregularities and missing timestamps were occasionally present.
These features included both time-varying telemetry data, such as rotational speed and power output, as well as categorical and static attributes, including turbine site, model, and geographic coordinates.

To focus the modeling efforts on the most informative signals, a feature selection process was carried out in consultation with a domain expert. Based on the input, and further guided by theoretical and empirical considerations, six features were selected for inclusion in the final model: average power output (\texttt{avgpower}), average rotor speed (\texttt{avgrotorspeed}), average wind speed (\texttt{avgwindspeed}), air density (\texttt{density}), ambient temperature (\texttt{ambienttemperature}), and average wind direction (\texttt{avgwinddirection}).

This selection is not only consistent with expert judgment but is also grounded in fundamental aerodynamic theory. As described by \cite{bilendo_normal_2022}, the power generated by a wind turbine can be expressed by the following equation:


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:power-curve}
P = \frac{1}{2} \rho A C_P(\lambda, \beta) v^3
\end{equation}
where \(P\) denotes power output, \(\rho\) is air density, \(v\) is wind speed, and \(C_P(\lambda, \beta)\) is the power coefficient, which depends on the tip speed ratio \(\lambda\) and blade pitch angle \(\beta\). Within this formulation, at least four of the selected features—namely, wind speed, air density, rotor speed (which influences \(\lambda\)), and power output—are explicitly involved, while ambient temperature indirectly affects air density. This theoretical linkage reinforces their relevance for modeling turbine behaviour.

The remaining 27 features were excluded from further analysis for several reasons. They consist of metadata—such as site identifier, manufacturer, model, latitude, and phase configuration—which do not change over time and are therefore uninformative for condition monitoring.

Focusing on a smaller, well-justified subset of features not only reduces the dimensionality of the input space but also mitigates the risk of overfitting and improves the interpretability of the learned models. This selection forms the basis for all subsequent preprocessing and modeling steps in this study.

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}
To ensure the raw telemetry data from EnergyPro was suitable for modeling and anomaly detection, a structured preprocessing pipeline was implemented. This pipeline addressed missing data, temporal inconsistencies, and varying feature scales through interpolation, resampling, normalization, and segmentation. The goal was to produce a clean, uniformly spaced time series with standardized feature values and robust labeling, optimized for downstream machine learning applications. The preprocessing steps are summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:preprocessing-pipeline}.


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{preprocess-pipeline.pdf}
    \caption{Preprocessing pipeline.}
    \label{fig:preprocessing-pipeline}
\end{figure}

The raw data consisted of timestamped multivariate measurements recorded at 10-minute intervals. However, due to occasional missing entries and timing irregularities, a time-based interpolation strategy was applied to recover a consistent temporal structure. Specifically, missing values were filled using time-aware linear interpolation, which estimates values at missing timestamps by weighting neighboring values according to their temporal distance. After interpolation, the data was resampled to an hourly resolution by averaging all available 10-minute values within each hour.

After temporal resampling and feature normalization, the data was segmented into overlapping windows of 128 consecutive hourly observations. Each window was then assigned a binary label (normal or abnormal) based on the point-level anomaly annotations provided by EnergyPro. To account for occasional labeling noise or transient misclassifications, a tolerance threshold was adopted: a window was labeled as abnormal if it contained fewer than 127 time steps labeled as normal. This decision was informed by empirical statistics—across all labeled windows, the floor of the mean number of normal-labeled steps per window was 127. In addition to its statistical grounding, this threshold also serves a practical purpose to reduce sensitivity to edge effects. At the onset or resolution of an abnormal event, the telemetry may still resemble normal operational patterns, despite the change in ground-truth labels. Allowing for one or two anomalous time steps to be discounted within a window ensures that the labeling remains robust to these boundary-phase ambiguities, which may not yet exhibit distinguishable signal characteristics.

Following this procedure, the resulting dataset exhibited a normal-to-abnormal class ratio of approximately 2.02:1.

To standardize feature magnitudes, a Min-Max scaling procedure was applied independently to each of the six selected features. 
Scaling bounds were determined using only the typical operating conditions of the wind turbines, ensuring that abnormal behaviour was not incorporated into the normalization statistics.
Min-Max normalization was chosen over standardization (z-score) due to its ability to preserve the original distribution and exact relationships between feature values—particularly important for anomaly detection tasks, where the relative spacing of signals may carry diagnostic significance. Moreover, such normalization has been shown to facilitate faster model convergence, reduce the risk of becoming trapped in local minima, and maintain interpretability across transformed features~\citep{fan_analytical_2018}.
For the circular feature wind direction, a sine-cosine transformation was applied prior to scaling to preserve its inherent angular continuity.
The operational ranges for the selected features, as observed in the dataset, are shown in Table~\ref{tab:feature-ranges}.

Finally, to prepare for supervised and semi-supervised learning tasks, the labeled dataset was stratified by class and split into training, validation, and test sets. For each class (normal and abnormal), data was randomly divided into 70\% training, 15\% validation, and 15\% testing subsets. In the unsupervised setting, abnormal samples were excluded from training entirely and retained only for validation and testing in equal proportions.

\section{Model Architectures}
\label{sec:models}

This section describes the components of neural architectures developed and evaluated for turbine health monitoring. All models share a common components but differ in their combinations and training strategies.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{model-generic-struct.pdf}
    \caption{Master diagram of architectural components. Each block corresponds to a named variant described in Table~\ref{tab:model-variants}.}
    \label{fig:model-components}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:model-components}, the general architecture is composed of three primary components: an encoder, a decoder, and a classification head. 
In addition, a reconstruction error is computed between the original input and its reconstruction, which can also be used for downstream classification or as a secondary objective. The calculation and usage of the reconstruction error varies across model variants and is discussed in Sections~\ref{sec:training} and \ref{sec:eval}.


\subsection{Encoder}
\label{subsec:encoder}
The encoder maps the input sequence into a compact latent representation, which is then used either for reconstructing the input via the decoder or for predicting anomaly labels via the classifier head.

Let $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T}$ represent the input time series, where $C$ is the number of channels and $T$ is the temporal length. The encoder $E$ maps $\mathbf{x}$ into a lower-dimensional space, producing a latent representation $\mathbf{z} = E(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times T'}$, where $d$ is the latent channel dimension and $T' < T$ is a compressed temporal length. In this work, $T'$ is typically $T/8$ due to progressive downsampling.

The encoder architecture is composed of a sequence of three sub-encoder blocks, stacked to progressively extract higher-level temporal features while reducing the temporal resolution. Two alternative designs for these sub-blocks are considered in this study: standard convolutional block and multi-scale attention block.

The first design is a conventional convolutional sub-encoder. Each block contains two convolutional layers: one for feature extraction and one for compression. These are followed by batch normalization and average pooling to ensure stability and temporal downsampling.

The second design draws inspiration from the MAMA (multi-scale attention memory) architecture introduced by~\citet{chen_mama_2021}, adapted here to 1D convolutional sequences. Each block integrates both temporal and channel-wise attention mechanisms. Specifically, temporal attention is computed across the entire sequence using position-wise query, key, and value projections, while channel attention is applied using learned convolutional projections.

The multi-scale block begins with a $1 \times 1$ convolution to extract temporal queries, keys, and values. These are used to compute temporal attention weights via dot-product followed by softmax normalization. The attended temporal output then undergoes a second stage of attention applied across channels, with the choice of attention style configurable as one of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Same Attention: standard attention with 1x1 kernel and stride 1.
    \item Down Attention: uses a 3x3 kernel with stride 2 to downsample the temporal dimension.
    \item Up Attention: uses a 3x3 kernel with stride 2 to upsample the temporal dimension.
\end{itemize}
For encoder, the down attention mode is used.


\subsection{Latent Representation}
\label{subsec:latent}
Two types of latent representations are considered in this study: a encoded vector $z$ and a hashed binary sequence $z_{binary}$.

The encoded vector $z$ is obtained by applying a Tanh activation function to the output of the encoder. The representation is kept in $\mathbb{R}^{d \times T'}$ to preserve the temporal information.

The hashed binary sequence $\mathbf{z}_{\text{binary}} \in \{0,1\}^{d}$ is inspired by the discrete memory mechanism in~\citet{chen_mama_2021}. The objective is to map latent vectors to their closest binary prototypes of normal behavior in a learned memory bank.

To obtain this binary representation, the latent vector $\mathbf{z}$ is passed through a hash projection function (a linear layer), then discretized using the sign function shifted by a small bias:


\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hashing}
\mathbf{z}_{\text{binary}} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \text{sign}(\text{fc}(\mathbf{z}) - 0.51) + 1 \right),
\end{equation}
where $\text{fc}(\cdot)$ is a learnable transformation, and the output is thresholded element-wise to yield binary values in $\{0,1\}$.

A memory matrix $\mathcal{M} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ stores $N$ prototype representations corresponding to learned normal patterns. The same hash procedure is applied to the memory vectors to produce $\mathbf{M}_{\text{binary}} \in \{0,1\}^{N \times d}$. Hamming distances between $\mathbf{z}_{\text{binary}}$ and each memory entry are computed as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}_{\text{Hamming}}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{binary}}, \mathbf{M}_{\text{binary}}) = \sum_{j=1}^{d} \mathbb{I} \left[ \mathbf{z}_{\text{binary}}^{(j)} \neq \mathbf{M}_{\text{binary}}^{(j)} \right],
\end{equation}
and used to retrieve the most similar memory entry.
The retrieved memory together with the latent vector $\mathbf{z}$ forms the final latent representation used for reconstruction.


\subsection{Decoder}
\label{subsec:decoder}

The decoder is tasked with reconstructing the original input sequence from the encoded latent representation. It complements the encoder and plays a central role in reconstruction-based anomaly detection, where the fidelity of $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ with respect to the original input $\mathbf{x}$ serves as a signal for abnormality.

Given a latent representation $\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times T'}$, the decoder $D$ maps it back to a sequence $\hat{\mathbf{x}} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T}$, approximating the original input. The decoder mirrors the encoder architecture and is likewise composed of three stacked sub-decoder blocks. Two decoder variants are implemented in this study to match the encoder styles:

The standard version employs transposed convolutional layers to incrementally upsample the temporal dimension and reduce the feature compression introduced by the encoder. Each sub-decoder block consists of 2 transposed convolution (deconvolution) layers, followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation, mirrowing the sub-encoder structure. 

When the encoder uses multi-scale attention blocks, the decoder adopts a parallel design using the same attention-based blocks but with upsampling configurations.

When the latent representation includes a hashed binary sequence $\mathbf{z}_{\text{binary}}$, the decoder is extended to incorporate this information as part of the reconstruction process, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:decoder-hashed}.

The reconstruction begins by concatenating $\mathbf{z}$ with $\mathbf{z}_{\text{binary}}$ and passing the result through a \emph{SA} block to facilitate interaction between the latent vector and the hashed memory. The resulting representation then flows through a \textit{UA} block to upsample the temporal dimension.

Simultaneously, the original latent vector $\mathbf{z}$ is processed through a separate \textit{UA} block. This output is concatenated again with the previous upsampled result. To enable further upsampling, the decoder applies a \textit{SA} block that reshapes the temporal dimension to match the previous upsampling stage.

The final reconstruction path consists of two additional \textit{UA} blocks applied sequentially. Unlike earlier stages, neither further combination nor attention reshaping is applied at the end of the decoder pipeline.

The series of model variants explored in this study emerged from a combination of empirical observations and hypotheses about how different architectural components might affect anomaly detection performance.

\subsection{Vanilla Autoencoder}
\label{subsec:vanilla-model}
The modeling process began with the implementation of a \emph{Vanilla Autoencoder}, a widely adopted baseline in unsupervised anomaly detection tasks, particularly in rotating machinery condition-monitoring applications~\citep{ahmad_autoencoder-based_2020,li_comprehensive_2023}. This architecture comprises an encoder-decoder pair trained to reconstruct its input time series data. The underlying assumption is that a model trained solely on normal operational data will generalize poorly to anomalous inputs, resulting in elevated reconstruction errors for such cases.

In this setting, reconstruction error served as the sole indicator of abnormality, with the model trained to minimize the reconstruction error between the original and reconstructed sequences, under the expectation that the autoencoder would internalize the structure of normal behavior and fail to reconstruct deviations from that norm.

However, in practice, this assumption did not hold. Experimental results showed that reconstruction errors were nearly indistinguishable between normal and abnormal samples. 
Inspection of the reconstruction outputs revealed that the decoder was often able to reproduce anomalous patterns with high accuracy, thereby undermining the discriminative power of the reconstruction loss. These limitations are further examined in Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

This outcome suggested that the encoder may not have learned a semantically meaningful representation capable of distinguishing between operating states. One possibility is that the latent space captured only generic temporal dynamics without encoding features sensitive to anomalies. These findings motivated the introduction of supervised components in subsequent models to encourage more structured and discriminative embeddings.


\subsection{Multi-Head Autoencoder}
\label{subsec:multihead-model}
To address the shortcomings of the Vanilla Autoencoder, a \emph{Multi-Head Autoencoder} architecture was introduced by augmenting the model with an auxiliary classification head, trained jointly with the reconstruction task. This modification allows the model to learn representations that are not only reconstructive but also predictive of the anomaly label.

The intuition behind this design is supported by prior work in semi-supervised learning, where label constraints have been shown to improve the clustering of latent features and enhance separation among different states~\citep{gao_newly_2021}. In particular, the classification head encourages the encoder to organize the latent space in a label-aware manner, leading to more structured and abnormal sensitive feature embeddings.

As expected, the reconstruction error in this model could now differentiate between normal and abnormal samples more reliably than in the vanilla setup. However, empirical results revealed a more surprising outcome: the classification head outperformed the reconstruction error in separating the two classes. While the reconstruction loss remained part of the training objective, it became increasingly evident that the classification output provided a stronger and more consistent signal of system health.

This observation raised a fundamental question about the necessity of the autoencoder itself. If the classification head alone could achieve strong predictive performance, then perhaps the reconstruction component was superfluous—or even detrimental—by introducing a competing objective. This hypothesis led to the development of the next model variant, which discards the decoder entirely in favor of a purely classification design.


\subsection{Classification-Only}
\label{subsec:classify-model}
Building on the insights from the Multi-Head model, the decoder is removed so the model can focus solely on differentiating the samples' status.

The motivation for this design was twofold. First, results from the Multi-Head variant indicated that the classification head alone was more effective than reconstruction error in detecting abnormal conditions. Second, removing the decoder simplifies the optimization process by eliminating competing training objectives, potentially allowing the encoder to focus exclusively on features relevant for classification.


\subsection{Bottleneck}
\label{subsec:bottleneck-model}
The final variant explored in this study, referred to as the \emph{Bottleneck} model, revisits the autoencoder structure with a significant shift in training objective. Rather than minimizing the reconstruction error uniformly across all samples, this model is explicitly trained to produce reconstruction errors that are \emph{discriminative} between normal and abnormal classes.

Structurally, the model retains the full encoder-decoder architecture of the Vanilla Autoencoder.
In addition, a simple MLP classification head is added on top of the reconstruction error to classify whether the error corresponds to a normal or abnormal sample. 
From this perspective, the architecture functions more like a supervised bottleneck model than a traditional autoencoder. Instead of treating reconstruction as an end in itself, the model is optimized to produce error patterns that aid classification.

\section{Training Procedure}
\label{sec:training}
This section describes the training objectives, loss functions, and optimization strategies used for the family of models introduced in Section~\ref{sec:models}. While all variants share a common encoder structure and input-output format, they differ significantly in how the loss is constructed and applied during training.


\subsection{Hyperparameters}
All models were trained using the Adam optimizer with initial learning rate of $1\times10^{-3}$ decaying at rate of 0.1 after 4 epochs the evaluation not improving and a batch size of 96. Models were trained for up to 100 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss with patience of 10 epochs. All training was conducted using the same data splits described in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.


\subsection{Loss Functions}
The loss functions used in this study can be put into two main categories by their purposes: \textbf{reconstruction loss} and \textbf{classification loss}. The reconstruction loss encourages the model to accurately reproduce the input sequence, while the classification loss optimizes the binary decision-making head. Depending on the model architecture, one or both losses may be present. When both are used, they are weighted equally unless otherwise stated.

For classification, I use the standard binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss, which measures the discrepancy between predicted probabilities and true labels. This encourages the model to assign high confidence to normal samples and low confidence to abnormal ones.

For reconstruction, the primary objective is to accurately reproduce the input time series. the mean squared error (MSE) is used as the base reconstruction loss, computed as the average squared difference between the input and its reconstruction. Additional distance-based losses such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), Wasserstein distance, and spectral-domain differences (e.g., Fourier loss) are also considered as auxiliary components to guide reconstruction quality. These are detailed in the~\ref{ch:related-works}

In this study, multiple loss functions are tested in support of the traditional MSE reconstruction objective. To combine MSE with an auxiliary distance metric, I adopt an uncertainty-based dynamic loss weighting scheme proposed by~\cite{yao_svd-ae_2024}. The total loss is formulated as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:auto-weight-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i} \frac{1}{2\sigma_i^2} \mathcal{L}_i + \log \sigma_i^2
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_i$ is the $i$-th loss term, and $\sigma_i^2$ is a learnable parameter that dynamically adjusts the contribution of each component during training. This formulation allows the model to automatically balance the importance of different reconstruction objectives—whether to prioritize exact point-wise matching of MSE, structural similarity, or frequency-domain alignment—based on their relative difficulty and uncertainty.
The considered auxiliary losses include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean Squared Error (MSE)}: primary reconstruction loss, measuring the average squared difference between input and reconstruction.
    \begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \hat{x}_i)^2
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)}: a kernel-based statistical measure used to quantify the difference between two probability distributions by comparing their mean embeddings in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS).
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{MMD}}^2(P, Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x, x' \sim P}[k(x,x')] + \mathbb{E}_{y, y' \sim Q}[k(y,y')] - 2\mathbb{E}_{x \sim P, y \sim Q}[k(x,y)]
    \end{equation}
    where \(k(\cdot, \cdot)\) is a kernel function.


    \item \textbf{Wasserstein Distance}: measures the minimum cost of transporting mass to transform one probability distribution into another, considering both the amount of mass and the distance it must be moved.
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{W}}(P, Q) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(P, Q)} \mathbb{E}_{(x,\hat{x}) \sim \gamma} [\| x - \hat{x} \|]
    \end{equation}
    where \(\Pi(P, Q)\) is the set of all joint distributions with marginals \(P\) and \(Q\).

    \item \textbf{Fourier Loss}: Operates in the frequency domain, comparing Fourier transforms of input and reconstruction to ensure spectral consistency.
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{Fourier}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left| \mathcal{F}(x_i) - \mathcal{F}(\hat{x}_i) \right|^2
    \end{equation}


    \item \textbf{Mahalanobis Distance}: Measures the distance between input and reconstruction distributions, accounting for correlations between variables.
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{Mahalanobis}} = \sqrt{(x - \hat{x})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \hat{x})}
    \end{equation}

\end{itemize}


\subsection{Reconstruction-Based Classification}
For model variants that use reconstruction error to identify abnormal conditions (e.g., Vanilla Autoencoder, Multi-Head Autoencoder, Bottleneck), two strategies are explored to map reconstruction behavior to binary predictions.

First, a static-threshold approach is applied, where the scalar reconstruction error (via loss function) is used directly as an anomaly score. A threshold is then selected based on validation performance by maximizing the AUROC. This method assumes that normal and abnormal samples yield statistically separable loss values.

Second, a learned classifier approach is tested. In this setting, the encoder and decoder are frozen, and a lightweight MLP is trained to classify normal versus abnormal samples using the reconstruction error vector as input. This allows the model to exploit finer-grained patterns in the error signal that a scalar threshold might miss, while avoiding interference with the main model parameters. It creates a dynamic threshold, which \cite{zhao_anomaly_2018} found to be more effective in reducing false positives.


\subsection{Training Strategies}
\label{subsec:training-strategies}
Each model variant in this study adopts a different training configuration based on its objective, particularly in how it utilizes normal and abnormal samples. These configurations reflect a progression from fully unsupervised to increasingly supervised learning regimes.

The \emph{Vanilla Autoencoder} is trained purely on normal data. This aligns with the classic unsupervised anomaly detection assumption: the model should reconstruct normal sequences well, while producing high reconstruction errors on unseen anomalies during inference.

For the \emph{Multi-Head Autoencoder}, both normal and abnormal samples are used during training. However, only normal samples contribute to the reconstruction loss—abnormal samples are excluded by masking their loss contribution. In contrast, the classification head is trained using all labeled samples. This setup allows the encoder to retain its reconstructive focus on normal data, while also learning discriminative representations through supervised feedback from the classification task.

The \emph{Classification-Only} and \emph{Bottleneck} model are trained using both normal and abnormal samples, with supervision applied solely through the classification loss. 

Additionally, a semi-supervised variation of the Vanilla Autoencoder, referred to as the \emph{Abnormal Reinforced Autoencoder}, is also explored. In this model, abnormal samples are included during training, but instead of minimizing their reconstruction loss, the loss function is inverted to reward high reconstruction error on anomalies. Specifically, the total loss is computed as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reinforced-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{normal}} + \frac{1}{1 + \mathcal{L}_{\text{abnormal}}}
\end{equation}
This encourages the model to reinforce a separation in reconstruction behavior between normal and abnormal inputs, effectively pushing the autoencoder to "unlearn" anomalies.

To enhance the classification performance, a shallow MLP is trained on top of the reconstruction error vector. 
This MLP is trained in a supervised manner using both normal and abnormal samples, with the goal of learning a more adaptive decision boundary that can better distinguish between the two classes based on the reconstruction error patterns.
The main model is first trained to reconstruct normal sequences, and then the MLP is trained with the frozen encoder and decoder. 
The MLP takes the reconstruction error as input and outputs a probability score for each sample, acting as a dynamic threshold for classification.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
Model evaluation follows a two-step pipeline: obtaining an \emph{anomaly score} for each input sequence, and applying a decision \emph{threshold} to classify it as normal or abnormal. For classification-based models, the prediction is taken directly from the output. For reconstruction-based models, additional scoring and thresholding steps are applied.

The anomaly score $s(x)$ is computed in one of two ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Loss-based scoring:}
The reconstruction loss itself is used as the anomaly score: $s(x) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}}(x, \hat{x})$,where $\hat{x}$ is the reconstructed input. The final binary decision is based on a threshold $\tau$. In loss-based scoring, this threshold is selected via validation to maximize the F1 score, defined as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:f1-threshold}
\hat{\tau} = \arg\max_{\tau} \ \mathrm{F1}(\tau).
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Classifier-on-loss scoring:}  
A shallow MLP is trained on top of features such as reconstruction error patterns, while the main model is frozen. The MLP outputs a class probability \( p(y=1|x) \), serving as the anomaly score. This supervised post-processing enables more adaptive behavior, including dynamic thresholding.

\end{enumerate}

Once a threshold is established, standard classification metrics—accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC—are computed to assess model performance.
These metrics allow comparison across models with different output modalities and are further analyzed in Section~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Experimental Environment}
\label{sec:exp-env}

All models were implemented using \texttt{PyTorch} (v\texttt{2.5.1}) and trained on a machine equipped with an \texttt{NVIDIA Quadro T2000} GPU and an \texttt{Intel i9-9880H} CPU. Training and evaluation were conducted under \texttt{Python} (v\texttt{3.12}) on a \texttt{Linux} system. Core dependencies included \texttt{NumPy} (v\texttt{2.2.4}) and \texttt{scikit-learn} (v\texttt{1.10.1}).
