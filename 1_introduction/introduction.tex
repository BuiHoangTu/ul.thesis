Following global commitments to Net Zero\webfootnote{https://netzeroclimate.org/what-is-net-zero-2/}{August 13, 2025}, renewable energy sources are playing an increasingly vital role in the modern energy mix.
Among these, wind energy stands out as a key contributor due to its scalability, cost-effectiveness, and minimal carbon footprint.
According to the Wind Report 2025~\citep{henrique_global_nodate}, global wind power capacity saw the addition of 117 GW in 2024, bringing the total to 1,136 GW—a year-over-year increase of 11\%.
The report further projects an average annual growth rate of 8.8\% through 2030.

Primary wind power expenditures lie in installation and ongoing maintenance~\citep{charabi_wind_2020}. 
Thanks to technological advancements, installation costs are steadily declining; however, maintenance costs remain a growing concern.
Extensive research such as the one by~\cite{yang_operations_2021} has focused on optimizing maintenance scheduling to mitigate these costs.
According to~\citep{dao_wind_2019}, maintenance can account for approximately 20\% to 30\% of the total cost of wind energy production.

Wind turbines are increasingly being installed in remote and offshore locations, where accessibility is limited and environmental conditions are more extreme~\citep{henrique_global_nodate}.
While such placements reduce installation costs per kWh due to higher wind and turbine placement availability, they simultaneously make maintenance operations significantly more expensive and logistically challenging.

This context has accelerated the shift toward condition-based maintenance strategies supported by telemetry and remote monitoring. 
Modern \gls{scada} systems now generate high-frequency time-series data from various turbine components, enabling what is commonly referred to as \gls{nbm} — the task of modeling normal operational patterns to detect abnormal events.
A variety of approaches exist for this task, ranging from statistical models to advanced deep learning architectures.
The technical foundations of these methods, along with their advantages and limitations, are reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}.


\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}
In this work, we adopt an \gls{ae}-based methodology due to their ability to learn compact representations of high-dimensional input data without requiring labeled anomalies.
Typically, an \gls{ae} is trained solely on data collected during normal turbine operation, under the assumption that it will struggle to accurately reconstruct anomalous inputs.
This allows the model to detect novel anomalies, which is the common case in wind turbine condition monitoring.
However, authors usually assume a small portion of the data is anomalous (e.g., 5--10\%)~\citep{fan_analytical_2018}, which makes it difficult to compare different methods in a standardized and reproducible manner.

%juan Mention the fact that current research often relies on toy datasets or very controlled setups
In recent years, labeled data has become more available, enabling the use of supervised training signals to improve anomaly detection performance.
The approach ranges from traditional supervised learning methods like SVM or decision trees to more advanced techniques like transfer learning and semi-supervised learning.
That said, \glspl{ae} remain a popular choice due to their ability to detect novel anomalies.
However, there still lacks a systematic exploration of how different \gls{ae} architectures and training strategies affect anomaly detection performance, especially in the context of wind turbine condition monitoring.

By systematically comparing Autoencoder architectures under realistic industrial conditions, this work aims to identify strategies that can be directly implemented in operational wind farms to reduce unplanned maintenance costs.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}
Despite the increasing use of machine learning techniques for wind turbine condition monitoring, there remains a critical gap in how anomaly detection models—particularly \glspl{ae}—are evaluated and benchmarked.
Existing studies often rely on strong assumptions about the anomaly rate in training data (e.g., 5-10\%) or lab experiments where the systems operated until failure.

These conditions can vary significantly in real-world deployments and make cross-study comparisons unreliable.

Moreover, while various \gls{ae} architectures and training strategies (e.g., unsupervised, semi-supervised) have been proposed, there has been no systematic investigation into how these differences affect anomaly detection performance in the wind energy domain.
This lack of rigorous comparative analysis impedes the development of robust, generalizable models and slows down the deployment of \gls{ml}-based predictive maintenance systems in industry.

Addressing this issue is essential to improving model reliability, reducing operational costs, and supporting the broader adoption of wind energy.
Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by providing a comprehensive evaluation of \gls{ae}-based approaches under varying data conditions and training paradigms, with the goal of identifying optimal strategies for \gls{nbm} in wind turbines.

\section{Aims and Contributions}
\label{sec:aims}
This dissertation revisits \gls{ae}-based anomaly detection within the context of wind turbine condition monitoring, leveraging a proprietary, labeled dataset provided by an industry partner.
This unique access enables a more realistic and systematic evaluation of \gls{ae} behaviour, addressing key limitations found in prior research that relied heavily on synthetic assumptions or unlabeled datasets.
By doing so, this work aims to bridge the gap between academic research and industrial deployment.

Firstly, the study investigates how Autoencoders reconstruct both normal and abnormal samples, providing insights into the reliability of reconstruction error as an anomaly detection signal in realistic industrial settings.
It also validates the common assumption that Vanilla \gls{ae} can effectively distinguish between normal and abnormal states with exclusive access to normal data.

Secondly, the research explores the impact of latent representation size and architecture on anomaly detection performance.
In addition, the separation of normal and abnormal with the help of a secondary classification head is examined.

Thirdly, the study compares multiple reconstruction loss functions.
While \gls{mse} remains the most commonly used loss function in the literature, several works have suggested incorporating secondary loss terms to capture additional characteristics of the data, such as distributional or frequency-based properties.
Accordingly, this study evaluates a range of supplementary loss functions to complement \gls{mse}, including \gls{mmd}, Wasserstein distance, and frequency-domain metrics derived through Fourier transformation.

Finally, the dissertation investigates the importance of \glspl{ae} in supervised anomaly detection settings, with a particular focus on the integration of classification heads and dynamic thresholding strategies.
This includes a detailed analysis of how different \gls{ae} components influence anomaly detection performance.
